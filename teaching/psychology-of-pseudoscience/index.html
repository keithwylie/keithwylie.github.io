pseudoscience

critical thinking and scientific literacy in psychology

Psychology of Pseudoscience examines why questionable claims can feel so compelling and why scientific thinking can be harder than it looks. The course uses psychology to understand paranormal beliefs, misinformation, conspiracy thinking, dubious health claims, forensic pseudoscience, and popular misunderstandings of mental health.

Students practice separating claims from evidence, identifying cognitive biases and logical fallacies, evaluating scientific sources, and communicating evidence to broad audiences. A central theme is that critical thinking is not just about debunking strange beliefs; it is also about understanding why people believe, share, market, and defend claims that outpace the evidence.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  • Identify cognitive biases, logical fallacies, and social influences that contribute to belief in pseudoscientific claims.
  • Differentiate scientific, pseudoscientific, and non-scientific claims using principles of critical thinking, falsifiability, validity, reliability, and reproducibility.
  • Evaluate the quality of evidence for controversial claims in psychology, health, law, paranormal belief, and popular science.
  • Explain the potential consequences of misinformation, disinformation, and pseudoscience for individuals and society.
  • Communicate scientific evidence clearly and persuasively to non-specialist audiences.

Course Units

The course is organized around memorable case studies that ask students to apply critical thinking to claims that appear in everyday life, media, health, law, and popular culture. Units have included:

  • Zombie Ideas: Scientific and pseudoscientific claims that refuse to die, including myths about personality, learning, and human behavior.
  • We Can’t Trust Our World: Critical thinking, contextual information, and the difficulty of evaluating claims in messy real-world environments.
  • Caveat Emptor: Logical reasoning, publication controversies, and the responsibility to evaluate evidence carefully.
  • Disorder in the Court!: Forensic pseudoscience, including claims about polygraphs, offender profiling, and questionable law-enforcement practices.
  • We’re All Mad Here: Pseudoscience in mental health, including recovered memories, controversial therapies, and oversimplified explanations of psychological disorders.
  • Just Because We CAM Doesn’t Mean We Should: Complementary and alternative medicine, health claims, and the psychology of medical decision-making.
  • Infodemic: Misinformation, disinformation, health communication, and why misleading claims spread.
  • Let’s Stare at Goats: Psychics, psi phenomena, extraordinary claims, and replication problems.
  • Fringe Charismatic Groups, Cults, Faith Healing, and Conspiracies: Belief, persuasion, authority, and group influence.
  • Social Distancing Champs: Cryptozoology, UFOs, ghost stories, and the psychology of extraordinary experiences.

Teaching Approach

This course emphasizes curiosity, skepticism, and science communication. Students are asked to take strange, controversial, or familiar claims seriously enough to investigate them carefully, while still applying rigorous standards of evidence.

  • Article Autopsies: Students examine published studies, controversial findings, retracted papers, and non-reproduced effects to understand how science can go wrong and how self-correction works. Examples include Bem’s ESP paper, Clark et al.’s religiosity paper, and other cases where students evaluate theory, methods, evidence, inference, and scientific controversy.

  • Science Communication Project: Students create public-facing science communication products about pseudoscientific claims or misinformation. Formats can include posters, pamphlets, podcasts, websites, videos, letters to the editor, op-eds, documentary-style projects, one-act plays, or other creative formats. The goal is to translate psychological science for real audiences without condescension.

  • Book Club: Students choose a popular press science or pop-science book, prepare reactions and discussion questions, and participate in discussion groups. These conversations ask students to summarize arguments, evaluate evidence, critique rhetoric, and consider how science reaches public audiences.

  • Case-Based Skepticism and Reflective Journaling: Units are built around concrete examples such as forensic pseudoscience, complementary and alternative medicine, recovered memories, cryptids, UFOs, faith healing, and misinformation. Students keep a physical journal throughout the semester, using it to connect course topics to their own beliefs, reasoning habits, and development as critical thinkers.
