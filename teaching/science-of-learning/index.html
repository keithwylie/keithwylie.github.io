Science of Learning

Science of Learning (COR 134) is a general education course that introduces students to psychological research through something they’re already familiar with: studying. We examine how people learn and remember information, why some study strategies work better than others, and how students can use that research in their own coursework.

Flagler Core Literacy: Social Scientific Inquiry
Flagler Core Value: Transformative Learning

Official Course Description

Academic success often entails managing and mastering multiple courses covering a variety of topics, each with their own unique requirements. Given these demands, the need to study and learn effectively is part-and-parcel of the life of any student. However, what methods entail studying effectively versus ineffectively? For example, is learning based on the amount of time a student spends studying? Is simply underlining or highlighting an effective method of learning? Is it better to study by reviewing notes or to test yourself? Should you wait and study for an exam right before you take it, or is it better to study in small chunks leading up to the exam? In this course, we are going to pose questions about studying and discuss the answers that have been uncovered through scientific research on human memory.

The Social Scientific Inquiry component asks students to think about how psychologists develop research questions, study human behavior, and evaluate evidence. Transformative Learning is especially relevant here because students use those findings to reconsider their own approaches to learning. They try research-based strategies, reflect on how things went, and decide what to change.

How I Teach the Course

Students read Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning by Brown, Roediger, and McDaniel alongside empirical articles on memory, metacognition, and learning.

In the face-to-face course, most weeks follow a familiar rhythm. Mondays introduce the week’s topic and the research behind it. Wednesdays are for reflection and discussion, often in small groups. Fridays are devoted to Research Friday, when students work directly with scientific literature and practice the skills involved in finding, reading, and evaluating it.

Students also develop personalized study plans. They apply ideas from the course to their other classes, receive feedback, and revise their plans later in the semester. Their final presentations give them a chance to talk about what worked, what didn’t, and what they would do differently.

Research Friday

Research Friday grew out of a familiar problem: we expect undergraduates to work with scientific research, but many have had relatively little practice reading an empirical article or using scholarly databases.

These short, recurring assignments give students that practice. We start by working through the structure of an article and identifying its research questions, methods, and findings. Over the semester, students become more independent. They search for relevant studies, compare evidence, evaluate competing claims, and consider how research applies outside the classroom.

Librarians are important partners in this work. Their expertise helps students become more comfortable with scholarly databases, search strategies, and evaluating sources.

Research Friday Materials

I’ve shared a selection of the worksheets I’ve used in Science of Learning, in roughly the order students encounter them. Some provide quite a bit of guidance; later ones ask students to do more on their own. The worksheets show how I build new skills while continuing to practice earlier ones.

Each assignment is available as a PDF for reading in the browser and an editable Word document for downloading and adapting. You’re welcome to use or modify them for your own classes. A few reference particular readings or library resources, so you may need to make adjustments.

The two note-taking worksheets are an example of how I’ve revised this approach. My original Research Brief asked students to find a follow-up study fairly early in the semester. When I taught the course again, I realized they needed more structured practice with the assigned article first, so I developed the Research Friday V2 version.
