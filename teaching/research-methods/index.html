research methods

how psychologists ask questions and build evidence

Research Methods is one of my signature courses and one of the most important experiences in the psychology major. The course introduces students to the logic and practice of psychological science: how to ask answerable questions, evaluate evidence, design studies, analyze data, and communicate findings clearly.

Across different versions of the course, I have taught Research Methods as a one-semester integrated course and as a two-course sequence. The throughline is the same: students learn research methods by doing research. They move from reading and evaluating published studies to designing research questions, collecting data, analyzing results, writing APA-style manuscripts, and presenting their work to academic audiences.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the course sequence, students will be able to:

  • Evaluate psychological research by identifying methodological strengths, limitations, validity threats, and ethical concerns.
  • Develop research questions and hypotheses grounded in psychological theory and prior literature.
  • Design descriptive, correlational, and experimental studies using appropriate controls, measures, and sampling strategies.
  • Analyze and interpret data using statistical software, including descriptive statistics, correlations, t tests, and ANOVAs.
  • Communicate research findings in APA-style reports, manuscripts, posters, and oral presentations.

Course Structure

The course is organized around two connected arcs: descriptive research methods and experimental research methods.

  • Descriptive Research Methods: Students learn how psychologists describe behavior, evaluate sources, review literature, measure variables, sample participants, design surveys, and analyze relationships among variables. Major topics include literature review, measurement, sampling, survey design, descriptive statistics, correlation, simple regression, and validity.

  • Collaborative Correlational Project: Students work in groups to design and complete an in-class survey study based on shared prompts and student interests. They define variables, write and revise survey questions, collect data, analyze correlations, create figures or tables, and report their findings in APA style.

  • Experimental Research Methods: Students build from research question to experiment. Major topics include independent and dependent variables, comparison groups, confounds, internal and external validity, one-factor designs, factorial designs, t tests, ANOVAs, interpretation of statistical output, and research ethics.

  • Independent Experimental Project: In the full experimental version of the course, students design and conduct their own IRB-approved experimental projects. They develop hypotheses, review literature, build materials, collect data, analyze results, write full APA-style manuscripts, and present academic posters.

Teaching Approach

This course treats research methods as an apprenticeship in psychological science. Students do not simply memorize design terminology or statistical formulas; they practice the habits of mind and practical skills researchers use when building evidence.

  • Scaffolded Project Design: The research process is broken into manageable pieces: topic selection, hypothesis development, literature review, measurement, survey construction, IRB preparation, data analysis, manuscript writing, and presentation design.

  • Collaborative and Independent Research: Students first practice research decisions through structured group projects, then move toward independent experimental proposals or full experimental projects. This balance lets students learn from peers while also developing ownership over their own research questions.

  • Statistics as Communication: Statistical work emphasizes choosing appropriate analyses, interpreting output, creating graphs and tables, and explaining results clearly. Students use statistical software to move from data to evidence, not merely from numbers to p values.

  • Authentic Scholarly Communication: Students write APA-style reports and manuscripts, create conference-style posters, and practice presenting results. Several student projects developed in this sequence have gone on to regional conferences, including the Great Plains Students Psychology Convention, and some have continued into peer-reviewed publication.

  • Research-to-Publication Pipeline: When projects show promise, I encourage students to revise their work for conference submission or publication. Ryan et al.’s article in the Journal of Psychological Inquiry is one example of a student project that grew from course-based research into a peer-reviewed article.

Tools and Open Resources

Students use accessible, open, or low-cost tools to support data analysis and research communication:

  • jamovi: Free, open-source statistical software with an interface that supports descriptive statistics, correlations, t tests, ANOVAs, regression, and data visualization.
  • Learning Statistics with jamovi: Danielle Navarro and David Foxcroft’s open statistics resource for psychology students and other beginners.
  • Open Stats Lab: Open statistics labs from Trinity University that support hands-on statistical learning.
  • APA Style: Guidance for manuscript formatting, citations, references, tables, figures, and reporting standards.

Signature Outcomes

Students leave the course with concrete, resume-ready skills: designing studies, evaluating methods, analyzing data, writing APA-style reports, collaborating on research teams, and communicating findings to scholarly audiences. For many students, Research Methods is also their first experience seeing themselves as contributors to psychological science rather than only consumers of it.
