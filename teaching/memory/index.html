memory

upper-level seminar in human memory, theory, methods, and applications

Memory is an upper-level seminar course for graduate students and advanced undergraduates. The course examines human memory from cognitive, neuroscience, applied, and methodological perspectives, with an emphasis on primary empirical research.

The course begins from the premise that memory is one of the most fundamental psychological processes: without memory, perception, learning, language, problem-solving, relationships, and social life would be impossible. Across the semester, students examine the diversity of phenomena organized under the heading of human memory, including theoretical models, laboratory paradigms, everyday remembering, memory development, metamemory, memory failures, and applied memory problems.

Because this course is structured as a seminar, students spend much of their time reading, discussing, and critiquing journal articles. Short lectures provide background and conceptual framing, but the central work of the course is collaborative analysis: students evaluate research methods, compare theoretical explanations, and consider how memory research can address real-world problems.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  • Describe major frameworks for studying human memory, including classic models and contemporary theoretical approaches.
  • Compare and contrast models of memory, including multi-store models, levels of processing, working memory, semantic memory, autobiographical memory, and applied memory frameworks.
  • Assess and interpret empirical research findings in memory science, with attention to methodology, theory, and evidentiary strength.
  • Evaluate and critique memory research through seminar discussion, short essays, and written synthesis.
  • Synthesize memory concepts to address real-world challenges in areas such as clinical practice, teaching and learning, legal psychology, health psychology, trauma, aging, and intervention science.
  • Design a research project applying memory science to a theoretical or practical question.

Representative Modules

The course is organized around seminar modules that connect classic theories, contemporary findings, and applied questions. Modules have included:

  • Classic models of memory and working memory
  • Levels of processing, transfer-appropriate processing, and retrieval practice
  • Semantic memory and knowledge structures
  • Autobiographical memory and flashbulb memories
  • Prospective memory and remembering to act
  • Metamemory, judgments of learning, feeling of knowing, and overconfidence
  • Memory development and childhood autobiographical memory
  • Memory disorders, dementia, traumatic brain injury, and clinical applications
  • False memories, recovered memories, and the “memory wars”
  • Trauma, stress, violence, and memory control
  • Alcohol, intoxication, and witness memory
  • Applied memory research in legal, clinical, educational, and health contexts

Teaching Approach

This course is built around seminar-style engagement with primary literature. Students annotate readings before class, lead discussion on selected topics, write short integrative essays, and develop a final project tailored to their academic level and field of study.

  • Discussion Leadership: Students lead seminar discussions by preparing questions, contextualizing assigned readings, and helping peers evaluate theory, methods, and applications.

  • Reading Annotations: Students annotate assigned readings to identify key claims, methodological concerns, theoretical connections, and questions for discussion.

  • Short Essays: Students write short essays that integrate, critique, or apply research from the course. Undergraduate students typically write shorter essays; graduate students complete more extended analyses.

  • Research Project: Students develop a final project connected to memory science. Undergraduate projects may include a book report, experimental proposal, or choose-your-own-adventure project. Graduate projects typically involve a research proposal or project relevant to their field of study.
