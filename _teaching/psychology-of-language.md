Psychology of Language is an upper-level seminar about psycholinguistics, sociolinguistics, and the ways language shapes cognition, social life, culture, and public policy. Students examine language as a psychological process: how people acquire language, understand speech, produce words and sentences, read, converse, and recover language abilities after injury.

The course also treats language as a social and cultural system. Students examine accents, dialects, linguistic discrimination, language change, new word formation, and the social meanings carried by everyday speech. Throughout the course, students connect language science to current events, public controversies, and legal or policy disputes. Examples have included why road sign fonts change, why seemingly small word choices can matter in Supreme Court opinions, and how words like “girlboss” and “adulting” enter the vocabulary.

Because this course is structured as a seminar, students read primary research from cognitive psychology, social psychology, psycholinguistics, sociolinguistics, neuroscience, and related fields. Class time emphasizes discussion, analysis, application, and critique rather than textbook coverage alone.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

- Describe major concepts and findings in psycholinguistics, including language acquisition, language processing, speech perception, production, reading, and conversation.
- Identify major brain regions and cognitive systems involved in language processing and explain how language can be affected by injury, development, and neurological conditions.
- Compare and evaluate psycholinguistic research methods used to study language perception, production, comprehension, and social meaning.
- Assess and interpret empirical research findings in language science, including work from cognitive psychology, social psychology, neuroscience, and sociolinguistics.
- Evaluate the social implications of language use, including accents, dialects, linguistic discrimination, language change, and the relationship between language and identity.
- Communicate language-science concepts clearly to professional and non-specialist audiences.

## Representative Modules

The course is organized around questions that connect cognitive mechanisms, social meaning, and real-world language use. Modules have included:

- Language acquisition and developmental milestones
- Speech perception, word recognition, and sentence processing
- Language production, conversation, and pragmatic meaning
- Reading, writing systems, typography, and road sign legibility
- Language and the brain, including aphasia and recovery after brain injury
- Bilingualism, second-language processing, and language learning
- Accents, dialects, linguistic profiling, and linguistic discrimination
- Language change, slang, neologisms, and new vocabulary
- Language, law, and public policy, including statutory interpretation and consequential word choice
- Language, technology, and artificial intelligence
- Language, culture, cognition, and social structure

## Representative Readings and Media

Required materials vary by semester and include empirical journal articles, edited chapters, podcasts, videos, and current-event analyses. The optional textbook provides background in psycholinguistics, but the central work of the course involves primary research and applied language analysis.

Students also complete a popular press book project. Book options vary, but the assignment asks students to evaluate a book about language using language science: What claims does the author make? What evidence supports those claims? What does the book communicate well to public audiences, and where does it oversimplify?

The course also includes _The Grammar of Happiness_, a documentary that introduces students to the Pirahã people of the Amazon and raises questions about language, culture, social structure, cognition, and linguistic relativity.

## Teaching Approach

This course emphasizes seminar discussion, primary-source analysis, and application to public life. Students work with empirical research, media examples, legal and policy controversies, and everyday language data.

- **Primary Research Seminar:** Students annotate, discuss, and critique empirical articles. They practice identifying research questions, methods, theoretical assumptions, evidence, and limits of interpretation.

- **Current Events and Language Analysis:** Students connect course concepts to ongoing public debates and everyday language examples, including legal interpretation, technology, education, social media, and cultural change.

- **Popular Press Book Evaluation:** Students select a popular press book about language and evaluate it through the lens of language science. This project emphasizes scientific accuracy, public communication, rhetoric, evidence, and audience.

- **Documentary Analysis:** Students use _The Grammar of Happiness_ to examine how language, cognition, culture, and social organization intersect. The documentary gives students a concrete case for discussing linguistic diversity and the challenges of interpreting language through cultural assumptions.

- **Discussion Leadership and Application:** Students help lead seminar discussions, prepare questions, synthesize readings, and apply research to examples from law, media, education, technology, and everyday communication.
