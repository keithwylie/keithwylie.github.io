Forensic Psychology examines how psychological science informs legal, correctional, and forensic practice. The course focuses especially on the roles psychologists play in legal settings, including forensic assessment, expert testimony, correctional treatment, consultation, and research-to-practice translation.

A distinctive feature of this course is its modular structure. Students complete a set of shared foundational modules, then choose elective modules based on their interests and professional goals. This structure reflects the breadth of forensic psychology as a field: some students are most interested in clinical assessment, others in correctional rehabilitation, policing, victimology, forensic science, or legal policy. The course gives students room to build a path through the field while still developing a shared foundation in ethics, evidence, and public-serving psychological science.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

- Identify psychologists’ roles in legal, correctional, and forensic settings.
- Distinguish between legal and psychological definitions of psycholegal concepts such as competence, insanity, responsibility, and risk.
- Evaluate the scientific foundations of forensic psychological claims, tools, and practices.
- Compare forensic assessment instruments and explain how they are used in legal contexts.
- Apply psychological research to improve outcomes for legal-system-involved individuals, including defendants, victims, incarcerated people, practitioners, and legal decision-makers.

## Course Structure

Students begin with required foundational modules, then select additional modules based on their interests. The course has typically included:

### Foundational Modules

- Forensic psychology: roles, settings, and professional boundaries
- Expert testimony and admissibility of psychological science
- Wrongful convictions
- Cognitive bias in forensic assessments
- Translating psychological science into policy and practice

### Elective Modules

Students choose from topics such as:

- Defining competence
- Evaluating competence with forensic assessment instruments
- Personality assessment in forensic contexts
- Competency restoration
- Correctional psychology
- Correctional rehabilitation and treatment
- Cognitive bias in forensic science
- Police psychology
- Forensic victimology

## Representative Readings

Because this course draws heavily from journal articles, professional guidelines, legal resources, and applied scholarship, readings change as the field develops. Representative readings have included:

- American Psychological Association. (2013). Specialty guidelines for forensic psychology. _American Psychologist, 68_(1), 7–19.
- Anderson, J. L., Plantz, J., Glocker, S., & Zapf, P. A. (2022). The MacCAT-CA and the ECST-R in competency to stand trial evaluations: A critical review and practical implications. _Journal of Personality Assessment, 104_(2), 211–223.
- Greenberg, S. A., & Shuman, D. W. (1997). Irreconcilable conflict between therapeutic and forensic roles. _Professional Psychology: Research and Practice, 28_(1), 50–57.
- Greenberg, S. A., & Shuman, D. W. (2007). When worlds collide: Therapeutic and forensic roles. _Professional Psychology: Research and Practice, 38_(2), 129–132.
- Neal, T. M. S., & Grisso, T. (2014). The cognitive underpinnings of bias in forensic mental health evaluations. _Psychology, Public Policy, and Law, 20_(2), 200–211.
- Neal, T. M. S., Slobogin, C., Saks, M. J., Faigman, D. L., & Geisinger, K. F. (2019). Psychological assessments in legal contexts: Are courts keeping “junk science” out of the courtroom? _Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 20_(3), 135–164.

## Teaching Approach

This course emphasizes flexibility, professional relevance, and applied scientific reasoning. Students work with empirical articles, professional guidelines, legal standards, case materials, and simulated forensic problems.

- **Modular Choice and Student Agency:** Students complete shared foundational modules, then choose elective modules aligned with their interests. A student interested in clinical forensic assessment might focus on competence, assessment instruments, and personality testing. A student interested in corrections might focus on competency restoration, correctional psychology, and rehabilitation. A student interested in policy might focus on cognitive bias, forensic science, victimology, or translating research into practice.

- **Ethics and Role Clarity:** Students examine the ethical and professional boundaries that distinguish forensic work from therapeutic work. Readings and activities ask students to identify role conflicts, confidentiality issues, duties to courts and clients, and the responsibilities that come with expert testimony.

- **Assessment and Evidence Evaluation:** Students compare forensic assessment instruments, evaluate the limits of psychological testing in legal contexts, and consider how courts decide whether psychological evidence should be admitted. Activities include evaluating Daubert-style questions, comparing competence instruments, and analyzing mock or real forensic evaluation materials.

- **Bias and Decision-Making:** Students examine how cognitive bias can shape forensic mental health evaluations, forensic science, expert testimony, and professional judgment. The course asks students to think not only about whether bias exists, but also how systems might be redesigned to reduce its influence.

- **Corrections and Public Good:** Students explore how psychological science can improve correctional systems, including treatment, rehabilitation, reentry, and competence restoration. The course emphasizes that forensic psychology extends beyond assessment and testimony; it also includes work that can benefit people involved in legal and correctional systems.

- **Independent Research and Application:** The final project asks students to investigate a forensic psychology topic of their choice using peer-reviewed empirical research. Students develop a scholarly bibliography, presentation, and paper focused on a topic that connects to their interests and possible future career paths.
