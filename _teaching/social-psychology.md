Social Psychology examines how people think about themselves, understand other people, and behave in social situations. The course focuses on the theories and research that help explain everyday social life: self-concept, social cognition, attitudes, persuasion, conformity, group processes, prejudice, discrimination, prosocial behavior, aggression, health, stress, and relationships.

A central theme of the course is that behavior makes more sense when we understand the person and the situation together. Students apply social psychological concepts to real-world issues such as policing, healthcare, mental wellness, discrimination, group decision-making, persuasion, and public communication.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

- Summarize major theories and empirical findings related to the self, social cognition, attitudes, persuasion, social influence, group processes, prejudice, discrimination, helping, aggression, and relationships.
- Explain how social situations shape behavior, judgment, emotion, and decision-making.
- Apply social psychological theory and research to real-world issues in areas such as health, law, education, workplace behavior, and public life.
- Evaluate examples of social behavior by identifying relevant psychological mechanisms, situational pressures, and methodological considerations.
- Communicate social psychological research clearly to broad, non-specialist audiences.

## Representative Topics

The course typically includes:

- Introduction to social psychology and research methods
- The social self, self-concept, and social identity
- Social cognition, heuristics, biases, and attribution
- Person perception, stereotyping, prejudice, and discrimination
- Attitudes, persuasion, and compliance
- Conformity, obedience, social influence, and group processes
- Prosocial behavior and helping
- Aggression, stress, health, and wellness
- Intimate relationships and social connection
- Science communication in social psychology

## Teaching Approach

This course emphasizes active learning, applied scenarios, and science communication. Students practice using social psychological concepts to interpret everyday behavior, analyze institutional practices, and communicate research to public audiences.

- **Applied Social Cognition:** Students generate their own examples or research paradigms for concepts such as overconfidence, counterfactual thinking, self-fulfilling prophecy, confirmation bias, hindsight bias, representativeness, belief perseverance, and actor-observer bias. These activities help students connect abstract theory to daily life.

- **The Social Self:** Students analyze their own self-concept through theory-driven activities. In one self-portrait assignment, students create a collage representing their self-concept and connect each image to concepts such as social identity theory, social comparison theory, self-discrepancy theory, or self-expansion theory.

- **Persuasion in Practice:** Students design persuasion campaigns by identifying the source, message, audience, and communication channel. They apply central and peripheral route tactics from the elaboration likelihood model and incorporate compliance strategies such as foot-in-the-door, door-in-the-face, scarcity, reciprocity, or social proof.

- **Group Decision-Making:** In the Lost on the Moon activity, students first make survival rankings individually and then work as a group before comparing their decisions to NASA rankings. The activity gives students a concrete way to analyze groupthink, process gain, process loss, expertise, conformity, and the conditions under which groups improve or undermine judgment.

- **Discrimination and Institutional Practices:** Students analyze scenarios involving policies or practices that may disadvantage particular groups, such as height requirements, legacy admissions, bail practices, inaccessible meeting spaces, or oral presentation requirements. The focus is on identifying whether institutional discrimination exists, what purpose the practice serves, and whether the same goal could be achieved in a less exclusionary way.

- **Prosocial Behavior and Helping:** Students apply theories such as social exchange theory, empathy-altruism, negative state relief, reciprocal altruism, kin selection, and social learning. They also use Latane and Darley's five-step model of helping to identify where helping can break down and how interventions might increase prosocial behavior.

- **Science Communication Project:** Students evaluate examples of science communication and then create their own public-facing piece about recent social psychology research. Formats can include op-eds, letters to the editor, blog posts, podcasts, documentary-style projects, or other accessible formats. The goal is to translate research accurately, clearly, and persuasively for a broad audience.
