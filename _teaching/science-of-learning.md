Science of Learning is a Flagler Core Curriculum course that introduces students to social scientific inquiry through a topic that matters immediately: how people learn. The course asks practical questions about studying, memory, attention, motivation, metacognition, and academic success, then uses cognitive psychology and social science research to answer them.

The course functions as a focused introduction to psychological science and research methods. Students learn how social scientists formulate hypotheses, collect data, evaluate evidence, and apply findings to real problems. Instead of treating learning strategies as tips or advice, the course asks students to examine the evidence behind them and test how those principles might improve their own learning.

## Flagler Core

- **Core Literacy:** Social Scientific Inquiry
- **Core Value:** Transformative Learning

As a Social Scientific Inquiry course, Science of Learning emphasizes how researchers ask questions about human behavior, gather empirical evidence, interpret findings, and apply evidence to improve real-world outcomes. As a Transformative Learning course, it asks students to use that evidence to rethink their own habits, assumptions, and approaches to learning.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

- Apply principles and methods from the social sciences to address questions, solve problems, or deepen understanding of human behavior.
- Explain how social scientists and cognitive psychologists formulate hypotheses, design experiments, and gather empirical data to study human memory and learning.
- Use critical thinking skills to analyze, compare, and evaluate study strategies and their underlying psychological and social science frameworks.
- Apply principles of memory and learning to real-world scenarios by identifying and implementing evidence-based strategies for effective studying.
- Synthesize research-based principles to develop personalized, effective study techniques and strategies.

## Materials and Representative Readings

The central course text is Brown, Roediger, and McDaniel's _Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning_. Students use the book as an accessible entry point into research on retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, desirable difficulties, fluency, metacognition, and transfer.

The course also uses empirical journal articles and research-based readings so that students can see how claims about learning are tested. Representative readings have included:

- Karpicke, J. D., Butler, A. C., & Roediger, H. L., III. (2009). Metacognitive strategies in student learning: Do students practise retrieval when they study on their own? _Memory, 17_(4), 471–479.
- Mueller, P. A., & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2014). The pen is mightier than the keyboard: Advantages of longhand over laptop note taking. _Psychological Science, 25_(6), 1159–1168.
- Articles on interleaving and retrieval practice in science learning.
- Articles on spacing and interleaving in applied learning contexts such as sports training, medical education, language learning, music instruction, art, and military training.
- Articles and critiques related to desirable difficulties, Dunning-Kruger effects, learning styles, growth mindset, motivation, and self-determination theory.

## Weekly Rhythm

Most weeks follow a three-day structure designed to move from concept, to application, to research practice.

- **Monday: Concept Kickoff:** Students begin the week with a focused introduction to the central concept, theory, or empirical finding. This day frames the question: What are we learning about learning, and why does it matter?

- **Wednesday: Reflect and Apply:** Students complete discussion-based or worksheet-based activities that ask them to connect readings to examples, evaluate study strategies, and apply concepts to real academic situations.

- **Friday: Research Friday:** Students work with empirical research, often through library databases or guided article-analysis worksheets. Research Friday is the signature component of the course: students practice using the library as a portal into social scientific inquiry.

## Research Friday

Research Friday scaffolds students' development as consumers of social science research. Early in the semester, students learn how to locate scholarly sources, distinguish empirical articles from other kinds of sources, and identify the structure of a journal article. Later activities ask students to summarize methods and findings, compare sources, evaluate evidence, synthesize across studies, and apply research to contexts that matter to them.

Examples of Research Friday activities include:

- **Reading a Scholarly Article:** Students work through Karpicke, Butler, and Roediger's article on retrieval practice and student study habits by identifying the research question, hypotheses, methods, results, discussion, and practical implications.

- **Taking Notes:** Students analyze Mueller and Oppenheimer's longhand-versus-laptop note-taking study and use it to evaluate their own note-taking strategies.

- **Spacing and Interleaving in the Real World:** Students use library databases to find empirical articles on spaced or interleaved practice in applied settings such as sports, medicine, music, language learning, art, or military training.

- **When is Difficulty Desirable?:** Students compare evidence supporting desirable difficulties with evidence that complicates the idea, then evaluate when challenge helps learning and when it may backfire.

- **Reheating Dunning-Kruger's Nachos:** Students compare the original Dunning-Kruger findings with recent empirical research that cites, supports, refines, or challenges the effect.

- **Learning Styles and Growth Mindset:** Students locate recent empirical studies on either learning styles or growth mindset, then pair with classmates studying the other topic to compare what the two literatures suggest for education.

- **Motivation and Learning Strategies:** Students find recent empirical work that includes both a motivational variable and a learning strategy variable, then evaluate whether motivation and strategy operate independently, reinforce each other, or interact in meaningful ways.

## Teaching Approach

This course uses the science of learning to teach the science of learning. Students practice retrieval, spacing, reflection, application, and metacognition while learning why those strategies work.

- **Research Literacy for Non-Majors:** Because many students enter the course without prior experience reading empirical articles, activities gradually build research literacy from the ground up. Students learn to identify research questions, participants, variables, designs, findings, limitations, and applications.

- **Learning by Applying:** Students connect course concepts to their own study habits, other classes, athletic or artistic practice, workplace training, and future professional contexts.

- **Study Plans:** Students develop personalized, evidence-based study plans, receive feedback, revise their strategies, and later reflect on what worked, what failed, and what they would change.

- **Library as Laboratory:** Research Friday positions the library as an active site of inquiry rather than only a place to find sources. Students use databases, citation details, article structure, and peer discussion to practice how social science knowledge is built.
