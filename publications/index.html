  1. What works and what doesn’t when interviewing in a second language
    Kaylee R. Dinwiddie & Keith Wylie
    Applied Police Briefings
    Abs DOI

    Witnesses and victims are sometimes interviewed in their second language, leading to social and communicative challenges. Complex interviewing strategies, including commonly recommended strategies like the reverse order technique, can hinder recall and reporting, whether the interview is conducted in the witness’s first or second language. Simple interviewing strategies, like asking repeated open-ended questions, generate lengthy, accurate reports from witnesses, even in their second language.

  1. Videos versus text spoilers and college students’ enjoyment of narratives
    Conner K. Ryan, Samuel Brown, & Keith Wylie
    Journal of Psychological Inquiry
    Abs DOI

    Common knowledge suggests that spoilers ruin stories for people—but is this really the case? In the current study, we investigated the effect of spoilers and narrative formats (video vs. text) on college students’ enjoyment of narratives. In this 2 (format: text, video) × 2 (spoiler: present, not) factorial study, Qualtrics randomly assigned 63 undergraduate students to one of four groups: short film without spoiler, short film with spoiler, text without spoiler, or text with spoiler. After either reading the short story or watching the short film, participants filled out a survey to measure their perceived enjoyment of the narrative. We expected to find that participants who watched a video with a spoiler would enjoy the video less than those that watched the video with no spoiler and those that read the text with a spoiler or without a spoiler. We found that spoilers did not reduce participants’ enjoyment of narratives and that there was no interaction between format and spoilers. However, we found a significant interaction between spoilers and format on participants’ reported processing fluency/plot comprehension, which could potentially explain our results. Finally, participants reported that they actively dislike and try to avoid spoilers, furthering the idea that people often over-forecast the spoiler effect. Spoilers are increasingly relevant and hard to avoid in today’s highly connected and media-focused society. Although research on the psychological effect of spoilers is limited and inconsistent, our findings suggest that people may overestimate the effect of spoilers on narrative enjoyment.

  2. The effects of a single-session pictorial collage on self-reported anxiety in adults diagnosed with a traumatic brain injury
    Bailey Bennett, Clara Corn, Gaelynn Wolf Bordonaro, & Keith Wylie
    The Arts in Psychotherapy
    Abs DOI

    Traumatic Brain Injuries (TBIs) are life-changing events that can contribute to the development of anxiety and depression, both of which are closely associated with poor overall outcomes. Brain injury may impact cognitive and physiological functions, regulation of mood, interpersonal relationships, personal identity, and self-esteem. Studies have demonstrated benefits of art therapy interventions with the TBI population that could help with recovery. The aim of this pilot study was to determine whether an art therapy intervention involving creating a collage could decrease self-reported anxiety in adult participants with a TBI. Through a quasi-experimental pre-test post-test single group design, the researcher gathered quantitative data using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). Results indicated a significant decrease in State Anxiety scores (t(7) = 6.76, p < .001) and a significant decrease in Trait Anxiety scores (t(7) = 3.06, p = .018) between the pre-and post-test. The results of this pilot study demonstrated a significant reduction of anxiety in participants following pictorial collage intervention in a single art therapy session. The potency of even brief engagement in art therapy experiences supports the use and benefit of art therapy with individuals with TBI.

  2. Investigating the utility of cognitive interview mnemonics among non-native English speakers
    Keith Wylie & Jacqueline R. Evans
    Psychology of Language and Communication
    Abs DOI

    Best practice eyewitness interviewing mnemonics have not been tested with linguistically diverse samples. Cognitively complex mnemonics may overload non-native speakers’ cognitive resources, which are already engaged in speaking a non-native language. Social facilitation mnemonics may help non-native speakers, who might be hesitant to report details. The current study tested the reverse order mnemonic and a set of introductory instructions compared to control interviews among native and non-native English speakers. Native speakers provided more details than non-native speakers, particularly in the control interview condition. Both the reverse order and control conditions elicited newly generated details because of repeated questioning. Accuracy rates were comparable across language and interview conditions. Future research should develop an interviewing protocol that is sensitive to the challenges faced by non-native speakers.

  3. The effects of face masks on emotional appraisal ability of children with autism
    Elizabeth K. Tate, Keith Wylie, & Jennifer D. Moss
    International Journal of Developmental Disabilities
    Abs DOI

    The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic drastically altered the ways in which people were able to communicate effectively. The addition of the face mask as a distracting stimulus interrupts the holistic process that people use to interpret facial expressions. The present study investigated the impact of face masking and gaze direction on emotion recognition in students with autism. Face masks reduced participants’ ability to emotionally appraise sad faces, and participants showed better accuracy appraising faces with a direct gaze. Exploring how face masks impact autistic individuals’ emotion recognition may benefit special educators and others who seek to improve communication with neurodiverse persons.

  1. Observers’ accuracy in detecting deception in non-native speakers versus native speakers: A systematic review
    Keith Wylie, Michelle M. Pena, Kevin B. Miller, Maley E. Palmer, & Elizabeth K. Tate
    Journal of Investigative Psychology and Offender Profiling
    Abs DOI

    As immigration and homeland security become of greater concern both globally and nationally, existing research on observers’ ability to accurately detect deception in non-native speakers warrants review. This systematic review summarized evidence on adult observers’ deception detection accuracy for non-native speakers compared to native speakers, identified response biases when judging native versus non-native speakers’ statements, and explored whether accuracy varies across targets’ proficiency levels. Eleven studies met inclusion criteria. Discriminability for non-native speakers was above chance in four samples. Most studies found support for a lie bias when judging non-native speakers and a truth bias when judging native speakers. The results highlight the need for better reporting of stimuli and observer samples, as well as standardized measures of language proficiency.

  2. A global experiment on motivating social distancing during the COVID-19 pandemic
    Psychological Science Accelerator Self-Determination Theory Collaboration
    Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences
    Abs DOI

    Finding communication strategies that effectively motivate social distancing remains a global public health priority. This cross-country, preregistered experiment tested whether messages emphasizing personal agency would be more persuasive than controlling messages. Multilevel regression results indicated that messages promoting agency increased reported intentions to engage in social distancing relative to controlling messages, particularly among those who had not been following social distancing guidelines. Effects did not differ significantly across country clusters, age, gender, or political ideology. These findings suggest that promoting autonomy is a promising approach for motivating adherence to social distancing guidelines across diverse populations.

  1. Pre-identification confidence is related to eyewitness lineup accuracy across witnesses with heterogenous encoding conditions
    Peter F. Molinaro, Steve D. Charman, & Keith Wylie
    Law and Human Behavior
    Abs DOI

    Eyewitness research has generally failed to show an association between eyewitnesses’ pre-identification confidence in their ability to identify the perpetrator from a lineup and their subsequent identification accuracy. This lack of association may be an artifact of methodologies in which witnesses experienced homogenous encoding conditions. The present research tested whether pre-identification confidence predicts eyewitness identification accuracy across witnesses who experienced heterogeneous encoding conditions. Across 1,236 participants, pre-identification confidence was significantly related to accuracy, with higher accuracy for confident participants. Participants who identified a suspect were more confident than those who did not identify anyone, but confidence did not differ significantly across lineup types or between correct and incorrect suspect identifications.

  1. Registered replication report on Srull and Wyer (1979)
    Randy J. McCarthy, John J. Skowronski, Katherine Hoogesteyn, Bruno Verschuere, Ewout H. Meijer, Ariane Jim, Steve D. Charman, Jacqueline R. Evans, Keith Wylie, E. Yildiz, & others
    Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science
    Abs DOI

    Srull and Wyer (1979) demonstrated that exposing participants to hostility-related stimuli caused them subsequently to interpret ambiguous behaviors as more hostile. This registered replication report tested the generalizability of that finding and examined whether working memory load or priming duration moderates the effect. Ten laboratories ran the experiment with a combined sample of 1,294 undergraduate students. Meta-analytic findings indicated that exposure to hostility-related primes significantly increased hostility judgments of the target person, with effects observed across eight of the ten laboratories. Working memory load did not moderate the effect.

  2. Registered replication report on Mazar, Amir, and Ariely (2008)
    Bruno Verschuere, Ewout H. Meijer, Ariane Jim, Randy J. McCarthy, Katherine Hoogesteyn, John J. Skowronski, Robin Orthey, Steve D. Charman, Jacqueline R. Evans, Keith Wylie, E. Yildiz, & others
    Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science
    Abs DOI

    The self-concept maintenance theory holds that many people will cheat to maximize self-profit, but only to the extent that they can do so while maintaining a positive self-concept. This registered replication report tested the generalizability of an effect reported by Mazar et al.: a moderate positive effect of activating the concept of dishonesty on dishonest behavior. Sixty-two samples, totaling 4,080 participants, conducted the replication. Meta-analytic results indicated that the mean effect size was significantly smaller than the original effect but in the same direction, suggesting that activating the concept of dishonesty may have a real but small effect on dishonest behavior.
